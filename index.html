Aquarius Project: Greenland ROV Survey

This survey was carried out under the Aquarius Project. Across the mission we surveyed an underwater volcano (the Caldera), an isolated seamount, two drowned valleys, and a fault line. This page brings it all together: a map of every dive we ran, an interactive 3D model of the ROV, and a gallery of imagery from the dives.

The GREAT project was completed under AQUARIUS-RI, exploring the Greenland Spreading Zone aboard the Marine Institute (Ireland) research vessel RV Celtic Explorer, together with a team of scientists from the Greenland Institute of Natural Resources, survey work led by Diana Krawczyk. The mission built a high-resolution picture of the entire Logi Ridge system and its inhabitants, and explored the western side of the Kolbeinsey Ridge axis within Greenland's EEZ: a heavily faulted seafloor dotted with volcanic edifices, and an abundance of feather stars.

For this mission the ROV carried a newly integrated instrument skid. Its hydraulic arms collected animals at depth and pushed cores to gather sediment, a Niskin bottle captured water samples, and a temperature sensor supplied by the science team was added to the unit. Two 4K cameras, one facing forward and one facing downward, recorded the seafloor throughout, with paired green lasers for scale.

Where we surveyed

ROV Survey Map

The map is locked to the Greenland–Iceland region so the sites sit in context. Each dive is a single marker, coloured by what it surveyed: the volcano (the Caldera), an isolated seamount, two drowned valleys, and a fault line. Click a marker to open that dive over its bathymetry, with the fixes we travelled fixed in place.

We originally read these valleys as glacier-carved, but the seafloor imagery points a different way. The flat summit and steep rim look like a surface planed by waves around a former sea level, while the valleys incised down the flanks appear to have started as subaerial gullies that were later reopened and deepened by submarine slope failures and sediment gravity flows once the surface drowned. On this reading an ice cap is not required to produce the morphology, so we now describe them simply as drowned valleys rather than glacial ones.
